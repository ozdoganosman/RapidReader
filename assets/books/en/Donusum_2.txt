Part Two
Franz Kafka

It was dusk before Gregor woke from his heavy, almost senseless sleep. He would certainly have woken not much later even without being disturbed, for he felt sufficiently rested and had slept his fill, yet it seemed to him that he had been roused by a fleeting footstep and by the cautious closing of the door that led to the front hall. The gleam of the electric tram lay pale here and there on the ceiling and on the upper parts of the furniture, but down below, where Gregor was, it was dark. Slowly, still groping clumsily with his feelers, whose worth he was only now learning to appreciate, he pushed himself toward the door to see what had happened there. His left side seemed to be one single long, unpleasantly tight scar, and he was forced to limp in earnest on his two rows of legs. One little leg, moreover, had been badly hurt in the course of the morning's events — it was almost a miracle that only one had been hurt — and dragged along lifelessly behind.

Only at the door did he realize what had actually lured him there; it had been the smell of something edible. For there stood a bowl filled with sweet milk, with little slices of white bread floating in it. He could almost have laughed for joy, since he was even hungrier than he had been in the morning, and he immediately dipped his head into the milk until it nearly covered his eyes. But he soon drew it back again in disappointment; not only did eating give him trouble because of his tender left side — and he could eat only if his whole body joined in, panting — but the milk, which had always been his favorite drink and which his sister had surely put there for that very reason, did not taste good to him at all; indeed, he turned away from the bowl almost with disgust and crawled back into the middle of the room.

In the living room, as Gregor saw through the gap in the door, the gas had been lit, but whereas at this time of day his father would ordinarily read the afternoon paper aloud in a raised voice to his mother, and sometimes to his sister too, not a sound could be heard now. Well, perhaps this reading aloud, which his sister was forever telling him and writing to him about, had lately fallen out of practice altogether. But all around, too, it was very quiet, although the apartment was surely not empty. "What a quiet life the family was leading," Gregor said to himself, and as he stared rigidly ahead into the darkness he felt great pride that he had been able to provide his parents and his sister with such a life in so fine an apartment. But what if all the peace, all the comfort, all the contentment were now to come to a dreadful end? So as not to lose himself in such thoughts, Gregor thought it better to get moving, and he crawled up and down the room.

Once during the long evening one of the side doors was opened a narrow crack and quickly shut again, and once the other; someone evidently felt the need to come in, but then again had too many misgivings. Gregor now stopped right by the living-room door, determined to bring the hesitant visitor in somehow, or at least to find out who it was; but now the door was not opened again, and Gregor waited in vain. Early in the day, when the doors were locked, everyone had wanted to come in to him; now that he had opened one door and the others had evidently been opened during the day, nobody came anymore, and the keys, moreover, were now on the outside.

Only late at night was the light in the living room put out, and it was now easy to tell that his parents and his sister had stayed up all this time, for, as could be clearly heard, all three of them were now moving away on tiptoe. Surely no one would come in to Gregor now until morning; he therefore had a long stretch of time in which to consider undisturbed how he was now to reorder his life. But the high, open room, in which he was forced to lie flat on the floor, frightened him without his being able to discover the reason, since it was, after all, the room he had lived in for five years — and with a half-unconscious turn, and not without a slight sense of shame, he scurried under the settee, where, although his back was a little squeezed and although he could no longer lift his head, he immediately felt very snug, and regretted only that his body was too broad to fit entirely under the settee.

There he stayed the whole night, which he spent partly in a half-sleep from which hunger kept jolting him awake, but partly in worries and vague hopes, all of which, however, led to the conclusion that for the time being he must keep calm and, by patience and the greatest consideration, make bearable for the family the inconveniences that in his present condition he was, after all, compelled to cause them.

Early in the morning, while it was still almost night, Gregor had an opportunity to test the strength of the resolutions he had just made, for his sister, almost fully dressed, opened the door from the front hall and looked in with suspense. She did not find him at once, but when she noticed him under the settee — good Lord, he had to be somewhere, he could hardly have flown away — she was so startled that, unable to control herself, she slammed the door shut again from outside. But as if she regretted her behavior, she opened the door again at once and came in on tiptoe, as though she were visiting someone gravely ill, or even a stranger. Gregor had pushed his head forward to just the edge of the settee and was watching her. Would she notice that he had left the milk standing, and by no means for lack of hunger, and would she bring in some other food that suited him better? If she did not do so of her own accord, he would rather starve than call it to her attention, although in fact he felt a tremendous urge to dart out from under the settee, throw himself at his sister's feet and beg her for something good to eat. But his sister noticed at once, with surprise, that the bowl was still full, with only a little milk spilled around it, and she promptly picked it up, not with her bare hands, to be sure, but with a rag, and carried it out. Gregor was extremely curious to see what she would bring instead, and he turned over all kinds of ideas about it. But he could never have guessed what his sister, in her kindness, actually did. To test his taste, she brought him a whole selection, all spread out on an old newspaper. There were old, half-rotten vegetables; bones left over from supper, coated in white sauce that had gone solid; a few raisins and almonds; a cheese that Gregor had pronounced inedible two days earlier; a piece of dry bread, a piece of bread spread with butter, and a piece of bread spread with butter and sprinkled with salt. Besides all this, she also set down the bowl, now probably intended for Gregor once and for all, into which she had poured water. And out of tact, knowing that Gregor would not eat in front of her, she left in great haste and even turned the key, just so that Gregor could see he was free to make himself as comfortable as he liked. Gregor's little legs whirred as he now set about eating. His wounds, besides, must already have healed completely; he no longer felt any hindrance; he was amazed at this and remembered how, more than a month ago, he had cut his finger ever so slightly with a knife, and how that wound had still hurt him quite enough only the day before yesterday. "Could it be that I have less feeling now?" he thought, and was already sucking greedily at the cheese, which, above all the other foods, had drawn him at once and insistently. Quickly, one after the other, his eyes watering with contentment, he devoured the cheese, the vegetables and the sauce; the fresh foods, by contrast, did not appeal to him; he could not even stand their smell and even dragged the things he wanted to eat a little distance away from them. He had long since finished everything and was just lying lazily in the same spot when his sister slowly turned the key as a sign that he should withdraw. This startled him at once, even though he was nearly asleep, and he hurried back under the settee. But it took great self-control for him to stay under the settee even for the short time his sister was in the room, for the plentiful meal had made his body swell out somewhat, and in that tight space he could scarcely breathe. Amid little fits of suffocation, his eyes bulging slightly, he watched his unsuspecting sister sweep up with a broom not only the leftovers but even the foods Gregor had not touched at all, as if these too were no longer of any use, and watched her hastily tip everything into a pail, which she closed with a wooden lid, whereupon she carried it all out. She had barely turned her back when Gregor was already pulling himself out from under the settee, stretching and puffing himself up.

In this way Gregor now received his food every day, once in the morning, while his parents and the maid were still asleep, and the second time after the family's midday meal, for then his parents likewise took a short nap, and his sister sent the maid off on some errand or other. They too certainly did not want Gregor to starve, but perhaps they could not have endured knowing more about his eating than what they heard secondhand; perhaps, too, his sister wanted to spare them what might have been only a small sorrow, for they were in fact suffering quite enough already.

By what excuses the doctor and the locksmith had been got out of the apartment on that first morning, Gregor was never able to learn, for since he could not be understood, it occurred to no one, not even to his sister, that he could understand the others, and so, when his sister was in his room, he had to be content with hearing now and then her sighs and her invocations of the saints. Only later, when she had grown a little accustomed to everything — there could of course never be any question of growing completely accustomed — did Gregor sometimes catch a remark that was kindly meant or could be interpreted that way. "He liked it today, though," she would say when Gregor had made serious inroads into the food, while in the opposite case, which gradually recurred more and more often, she used to say, almost sadly: "Now it's all been left again."

But while Gregor could learn no news directly, he overheard a good deal from the adjoining rooms, and whenever he did hear voices, he would run straight to the door in question and press his whole body against it. Especially in the early days there was no conversation that was not somehow about him, even if only covertly. For two days, at every meal, one could hear them deliberating over how they ought to behave now; but between meals, too, they talked about the same subject, for there were always at least two members of the family at home, since presumably no one wanted to stay at home alone, and they could on no account leave the apartment entirely empty. Moreover, on the very first day the maid — it was not quite clear what, or how much, she knew of what had happened — had begged his mother on her knees to dismiss her at once, and when she took her leave a quarter of an hour later, she thanked them for the dismissal with tears, as if for the greatest kindness ever done her in this house, and, without anyone asking it of her, swore a terrible oath not to betray the slightest thing to anyone.

Now his sister, together with his mother, had to do the cooking as well; admittedly that was not much trouble, for they ate almost nothing. Again and again Gregor heard one of them urging another to eat, in vain, and receiving no other answer than "Thanks, I've had enough," or something similar. Perhaps they drank nothing either. Often his sister asked his father whether he would like some beer, and warmly offered to fetch it herself, and when his father said nothing, she told him, to remove any scruple he might have, that she could also send the concierge for it; but then at last his father said a loud "No," and nothing more was said about it.

In the course of that very first day his father laid out the family's entire financial circumstances and prospects to both his mother and his sister. Every now and then he got up from the table and fetched some receipt or notebook from his small Wertheim safe, which he had rescued from the collapse of his business five years before. One could hear him unlocking the complicated lock and, once he had taken out what he was looking for, locking it again. These explanations of his father's were in part the first cheering thing Gregor had heard since his captivity began. He had been of the opinion that nothing whatever had been left to his father from that business; at least his father had told him nothing to the contrary, though Gregor, admittedly, had never asked him about it either. Gregor's only concern back then had been to do everything in his power to make the family forget, as quickly as possible, the business disaster that had plunged them all into utter hopelessness. And so he had begun to work at that time with quite particular fire, and almost overnight had risen from a minor clerk to a traveling salesman, who naturally had entirely different opportunities for earning money, and whose successes at work were turned at once, in the form of commission, into cash that could be laid on the table at home before the astonished and delighted family. Those had been fine times, and never afterward had they come again, at least not with the same luster, although Gregor later earned so much money that he was able to bear the expenses of the whole family, and did bear them. They had simply grown used to it, the family as well as Gregor; the money was accepted gratefully, he handed it over gladly, but no special warmth would come of it anymore. Only his sister had still remained close to Gregor, and it was his secret plan to send her, who unlike Gregor loved music dearly and could play the violin movingly, to the conservatory next year, regardless of the great expense this would inevitably entail, which could surely be made up in some other way. Often during Gregor's short stays in the city the conservatory came up in his conversations with his sister, but always only as a beautiful dream whose realization was not to be thought of, and his parents did not like to hear even these innocent mentions; but Gregor had it very firmly in mind and intended to announce it solemnly on Christmas Eve.

Such thoughts, entirely useless in his present condition, went through his head as he clung there upright against the door, listening. Sometimes, out of general weariness, he could no longer listen at all and let his head bump carelessly against the door, but he would at once hold it steady again, for even the small noise he had made in doing so had been heard next door and had silenced them all. "What's he up to now," said his father after a while, evidently turned toward the door, and only then was the interrupted conversation gradually taken up again.

Gregor now learned more than enough — for his father tended to repeat himself often in his explanations, partly because he himself had not dealt with these matters for a long time, partly also because his mother did not understand everything the first time — that despite all their misfortune a certain sum, admittedly a very small one, still remained from the old days, which the untouched interest had in the meantime allowed to grow a little. Besides this, the money Gregor had brought home every month — he had kept only a few gulden for himself — had not been entirely used up and had accumulated into a small capital. Gregor, behind his door, nodded eagerly, delighted by this unexpected prudence and thrift. Strictly speaking, he could of course have used this surplus money to pay off more of his father's debt to the boss, and the day on which he could have rid himself of this job would have been much closer, but now it was undoubtedly better the way his father had arranged it.

Now, this money was by no means enough for the family to live on the interest, say; it might have been enough to keep the family for one year, two at the most, but that was all. So it was merely a sum that really ought not to be touched, and that had to be put aside for emergencies; the money to live on would have to be earned. But his father, though healthy, was an old man who had not worked for five years and in any case could not expect much of himself; in these five years, which had been the first holiday of his toilsome yet unsuccessful life, he had put on a great deal of fat and had become quite sluggish as a result. And was his old mother now supposed to earn money, perhaps, she who suffered from asthma, for whom even a walk through the apartment was an exertion, and who spent every other day on the sofa by the open window, struggling for breath? And was his sister to earn money, who at seventeen was still a child, and who so thoroughly deserved the way of life she had led until now, which had consisted of dressing prettily, sleeping late, helping out in the household, taking part in a few modest amusements and, above all, playing the violin? Whenever the talk turned to this necessity of earning money, Gregor would always first let go of the door and throw himself onto the cool leather sofa that stood beside it, for he was burning hot with shame and grief.

Often he lay there through the whole of the long nights, not sleeping for a moment and only scraping at the leather for hours on end. Or he did not shrink from the great labor of pushing an armchair over to the window, then crawling up onto the sill and, braced against the chair, leaning against the window, evidently only in some sort of memory of the sense of release that looking out of the window used to give him. For in fact, from day to day, he saw things even a short distance away more and more indistinctly; the hospital across the street, whose all-too-frequent sight he used to curse, he could no longer make out at all, and had he not known for certain that he lived on the quiet but thoroughly urban Charlottenstraße, he might have believed he was looking out of his window onto a wasteland in which gray sky and gray earth merged indistinguishably. His attentive sister had needed to see only twice that the armchair was standing by the window before she took to pushing the chair back to exactly that spot by the window every time she had tidied the room, and from then on she even left the inner casement open.

If only Gregor had been able to speak with his sister and thank her for everything she had to do for him, he would have borne her services more easily; as it was, they pained him. His sister did try, of course, to smooth over the awkwardness of the whole thing as far as she could, and the more time passed, the better she naturally succeeded, but Gregor, too, came in time to see through everything much more clearly. Even her entrance was dreadful for him. No sooner had she come in than, without taking the time to shut the door, however careful she otherwise was to spare everyone the sight of Gregor's room, she ran straight to the window and tore it open with hurried hands, as if she were nearly suffocating, and stayed by the window a little while, no matter how cold it was, breathing deeply. With this rushing and clatter she frightened Gregor twice a day; the whole time he trembled under the settee, and yet he knew very well that she would certainly have been glad to spare him this, if only she had been able to stay in a room where Gregor was with the window closed.

Once, when a month or so must already have passed since Gregor's transformation, and there was really no particular reason anymore for his sister to be astonished at Gregor's appearance, she came a little earlier than usual and found Gregor still looking out of the window, motionless and posed there as if expressly to give a fright. It would not have surprised Gregor if she had not come in, since his position kept her from opening the window at once, but she not only failed to come in, she actually recoiled and shut the door; a stranger might well have thought that Gregor had been lying in wait for her and had meant to bite her. Gregor of course hid under the settee at once, but he had to wait until noon before his sister came back, and she seemed much more uneasy than usual. From this he realized that the sight of him was still unbearable to her and was bound to remain unbearable, and that she probably had to force herself hard not to run away at the sight of even the small part of his body that stuck out from under the settee. To spare her even this sight, one day, on his back — the job took him four hours — he carried the bedsheet over to the settee and arranged it in such a way that he was now completely covered and his sister could not see him even if she bent down. Had she thought the sheet unnecessary, she could of course have removed it, for it was clear enough that shutting himself off so completely could hardly be one of Gregor's pleasures, but she left the sheet just as it was, and Gregor even believed he had caught a grateful look when he once carefully lifted the sheet a little with his head to see how his sister was taking the new arrangement.

During the first two weeks his parents could not bring themselves to come in to him, and he often heard them expressing full appreciation of his sister's present work, whereas until then they had often been annoyed with her, because she had struck them as a somewhat useless girl. Now, however, both of them, father and mother, often waited outside Gregor's room while his sister was tidying it, and the moment she came out she had to tell them in precise detail what the room looked like, what Gregor had eaten, how he had behaved this time, and whether some slight improvement might perhaps be noticed. His mother, incidentally, wanted to visit Gregor comparatively soon, but his father and sister at first held her back with reasoned arguments, to which Gregor listened very attentively and of which he wholly approved. Later, however, she had to be held back by force, and when she then cried out, "Let me go to Gregor, he is my unfortunate son! Don't you understand that I have to go to him?" Gregor would think that it might be a good thing after all if his mother came in, not every day of course, but perhaps once a week; she understood everything much better than his sister, after all, who for all her courage was still only a child and in the end had perhaps taken on so hard a task only out of childish thoughtlessness.

Gregor's wish to see his mother was soon fulfilled. During the day Gregor did not want to show himself at the window, if only out of consideration for his parents, but he could not crawl very far on the few square meters of floor either; lying still was hard enough for him to bear during the night; eating soon gave him not the slightest pleasure anymore; and so, for distraction, he took up the habit of crawling crisscross over the walls and ceiling. He especially liked hanging from the ceiling; it was quite different from lying on the floor; one breathed more freely; a gentle swaying ran through one's body; and in the almost happy absent-mindedness in which Gregor found himself up there, it could happen that, to his own surprise, he let go and smacked down onto the floor. But now, of course, he had his body far better under control than before, and even a fall from such a height did him no harm. His sister noticed at once the new pastime Gregor had found for himself — he did, after all, leave traces of his sticky substance here and there as he crawled — and so she got it into her head to give Gregor as much room to crawl as possible and to take out the furniture that stood in his way, that is, above all the wardrobe and the desk. But she was not able to do this by herself; she did not dare ask her father for help; the maid would certainly not have helped her, for this girl of about sixteen, though she had bravely held out since the former cook was dismissed, had asked for the privilege of keeping the kitchen locked at all times and of having to open it only when specially called; so there was nothing left for his sister but to fetch her mother one day while her father was out. His mother did come, with exclamations of excited joy, but fell silent at the door of Gregor's room. First, of course, his sister checked that everything in the room was in order; only then did she let her mother enter. In the greatest haste Gregor had pulled the sheet down lower and into more folds; the whole thing really looked like nothing more than a sheet thrown carelessly over the settee. This time, too, Gregor refrained from peeking out from under the sheet; he gave up seeing his mother on this occasion and was simply glad that she had come after all. "Come on, you can't see him," said his sister, and she was evidently leading her mother by the hand. Gregor now heard the two frail women shifting the old wardrobe, which was heavy for all that, from its place, and heard his sister constantly claiming the greater part of the work for herself, ignoring the warnings of her mother, who was afraid she would overexert herself. It took a very long time. After perhaps a quarter of an hour's work, his mother said they had better leave the wardrobe where it was after all, for in the first place it was too heavy, they would not be finished before father came home, and with the wardrobe in the middle of the room they would block Gregor's way in every direction; and in the second place it was not at all certain that removing the furniture would be doing Gregor a favor. To her it seemed to be quite the opposite; the sight of the bare wall positively weighed on her heart; and why should Gregor not have the same feeling, since he had long been used to the furniture in his room and would therefore feel forsaken in an empty one. "And isn't it then," his mother concluded very softly, for she was almost whispering the whole time, as if she wanted to keep Gregor, whose exact whereabouts she did not know, from hearing even the sound of her voice, since she was convinced he did not understand the words, "and isn't it as if, by taking away the furniture, we were showing that we have given up all hope of improvement and are leaving him to himself without any consideration? I think it would be best to try to keep the room exactly as it was before, so that when Gregor comes back to us he will find everything unchanged and can all the more easily forget the time in between."

As he listened to these words of his mother's, Gregor realized that the absence of any human voice speaking directly to him, combined with the monotonous life in the midst of the family, must have confused his mind over the course of these two months, for he could not otherwise explain to himself how he could seriously have wished for his room to be emptied. Did he really want to have this warm room, so comfortably furnished with inherited pieces, turned into a cave in which he would, to be sure, be able to crawl undisturbed in every direction, but at the price of simultaneously, swiftly and completely forgetting his human past? He was already close to forgetting it now, and only his mother's voice, which he had not heard for so long, had shaken him awake. Nothing was to be removed, everything had to stay; he could not do without the good influence of the furniture on his condition; and if the furniture kept him from pursuing his senseless crawling about, that was no loss but a great gain.

But his sister, unfortunately, was of a different opinion; she had grown used, and not entirely without justification, to presenting herself to her parents as the particular expert whenever Gregor's affairs were discussed, and so now, too, her mother's advice was reason enough for his sister to insist on the removal not only of the wardrobe and the desk, which was all she had first had in mind, but of all the furniture, with the exception of the indispensable settee. It was, of course, not merely childish defiance and the self-confidence she had lately gained, so unexpectedly and with such difficulty, that led her to this demand; she had in fact observed that Gregor needed a great deal of room to crawl, while as far as one could see he made not the slightest use of the furniture. Perhaps, though, the romantic fervor of girls her age also played a part, which seeks its satisfaction at every opportunity, and by which Grete now let herself be tempted to make Gregor's situation even more frightening, so that she could then do even more for him than she had until now. For into a room where Gregor ruled the bare walls all alone, no one but Grete would ever dare to set foot.

And so she would not be talked out of her decision by her mother, who in this room, too, seemed unsure of herself from sheer agitation, soon fell silent, and helped his sister as best she could to get the wardrobe out. Well, if need be Gregor could do without the wardrobe, but the desk at least had to stay. And no sooner had the women left the room with the wardrobe, groaning as they pushed against it, than Gregor thrust his head out from under the settee to see how he might intervene, cautiously and as considerately as possible. But as bad luck would have it, it was his mother who came back first, while Grete, in the next room, held the wardrobe in her arms and rocked it back and forth on her own, without, of course, budging it from the spot. His mother, however, was not used to the sight of Gregor; it might have made her ill; and so Gregor, alarmed, scuttled backward to the far end of the settee, but could no longer prevent the sheet from stirring slightly at the front. That was enough to catch his mother's attention. She faltered, stood still for a moment, and then went back to Grete.

Although Gregor kept telling himself that nothing out of the ordinary was happening, that only a few pieces of furniture were being moved around, this coming and going of the women, their little calls to each other, the scraping of the furniture across the floor, affected him, as he soon had to admit, like a great commotion fed from every side, and however tightly he drew in his head and legs and pressed his body to the floor, he could not help telling himself that he would not be able to stand it much longer. They were clearing out his room; taking from him everything he held dear; the wardrobe, in which he kept his fretsaw and other tools, they had already carried out; now they were loosening the desk, which had dug itself firmly into the floor, and at which he had done his homework as a student at the commercial academy, as a pupil at the middle school, and even as a child at primary school — at this point he really had no more time to weigh the good intentions of the two women, whose existence, incidentally, he had almost forgotten, for they were so exhausted that they were already working in silence, and nothing could be heard but the heavy tread of their feet.

And so he broke out — the women were just leaning against the desk in the next room to catch their breath a little — changed the direction of his run four times, truly not knowing what to save first, and then he saw, hanging conspicuously on the wall, which was otherwise already bare, the picture of the lady dressed all in furs, crawled hurriedly up to it and pressed himself against the glass, which held him fast and felt good against his hot belly. This picture, at least, which Gregor now covered completely, no one would take away, of that he was sure. He twisted his head toward the living-room door so as to watch the women when they returned.

They had not allowed themselves much rest and were already coming back; Grete had put her arm around her mother and was almost carrying her. "Well, what shall we take now?" said Grete, and looked around. Then her eyes met Gregor's on the wall. It was probably only because her mother was there that she kept her composure; she bent her face toward her mother to keep her from looking around, and said, though trembling and without thinking: "Come, hadn't we better go back into the living room for a moment?" Grete's intention was clear to Gregor: she wanted to get her mother to safety and then chase him down from the wall. Well, she could always try! He was sitting on his picture and would not give it up. He would sooner leap at Grete's face.

But Grete's words had only made her mother all the more uneasy; she stepped aside, caught sight of the enormous brown blotch on the flowered wallpaper, and, before it really dawned on her that what she was seeing was Gregor, cried out in a shrill, hoarse voice, "Oh God, oh God!" and fell with her arms spread wide, as if giving up everything, across the settee, and did not move. "You, Gregor!" cried his sister, with her fist raised and a piercing stare. They were the first words she had addressed directly to him since the transformation. She ran into the next room to fetch some kind of essence with which she might rouse her mother from her faint; Gregor wanted to help too — there was still time to rescue the picture — but he was stuck fast to the glass and had to tear himself loose by force; then he too ran into the next room, as if he could give his sister some advice, as in the old days; but then he had to stand idly behind her; while she was rummaging among various little bottles, she was startled yet again when she turned around; a bottle fell to the floor and broke; a splinter cut Gregor's face, some corrosive medicine flowed around him; Grete, without lingering any longer, now took as many little bottles as she could hold and ran in with them to her mother; she slammed the door shut with her foot. Gregor was now shut off from his mother, who through his fault was perhaps near death; he must not open the door if he did not want to drive away his sister, who had to stay with his mother; there was nothing for him to do now but wait; and, beset by self-reproach and worry, he began to crawl, crawled over everything, walls, furniture and ceiling, and finally, in his despair, when the whole room was already beginning to spin around him, fell down onto the middle of the big table.

A little while passed; Gregor lay there limply; all around him it was quiet; perhaps that was a good sign. Then the doorbell rang. The maid, of course, was shut in her kitchen, and so Grete had to go and open the door. His father had come home. "What's happened?" were his first words; Grete's appearance must have given everything away. Grete answered in a muffled voice; evidently she was pressing her face against her father's chest: "Mother fainted, but she's already feeling better. Gregor has broken out." "I expected as much," said his father, "I always told you so, but you women won't listen." It was clear to Gregor that his father had misread Grete's all-too-brief report and assumed that Gregor had committed some act of violence. Gregor therefore had to try to calm his father down now, for he had neither the time nor the means to set him straight. And so he fled to the door of his room and pressed himself against it, so that his father, on coming in from the front hall, could see at once that Gregor had every intention of returning to his room immediately, and that there was no need to drive him back, that one had only to open the door and he would vanish at once.

But his father was in no mood to notice such subtleties. "Ah!" he shouted the moment he came in, in a tone that sounded at once furious and glad. Gregor drew his head back from the door and raised it toward his father. He had truly not pictured his father the way he stood there now; admittedly, absorbed of late in his novel crawling about, he had neglected to concern himself as he used to with what went on in the rest of the apartment, and ought really to have been prepared to find things changed. And yet, and yet, was this still his father? The same man who used to lie wearily buried in bed whenever Gregor set out on a business trip; who, on the evenings Gregor came home, had received him in his dressing gown in the easy chair; who was hardly able to get up at all, but merely raised his arms as a sign of joy; and who, on the rare walks they took together on a few Sundays a year and on the highest holidays, between Gregor and his mother, who walked slowly enough as it was, went along still a little more slowly, bundled up in his old overcoat, laboring forward and always planting his cane with care, and who, whenever he wanted to say something, nearly always came to a halt and gathered his escort around him? Now, however, he was holding himself very straight, dressed in a tight-fitting blue uniform with gold buttons, of the kind worn by attendants at banking houses; above the high, stiff collar of the jacket his heavy double chin spread out; from under his bushy eyebrows his black eyes looked out, fresh and alert; his white hair, usually tousled, had been combed down into a painfully exact, gleaming parting. He tossed his cap, which bore a gold monogram, probably that of a bank, in an arc across the whole room onto the settee, and, with the tails of his long uniform coat flung back and his hands in his trouser pockets, advanced on Gregor with a grim, set face. He probably did not know himself what he meant to do; in any case he lifted his feet unusually high, and Gregor marveled at the gigantic size of his boot soles. But he did not dwell on this; he knew well enough from the very first day of his new life that his father considered only the utmost severity appropriate where he was concerned. And so he ran ahead of his father, stopped short when his father stood still, and hurried forward again the moment his father so much as stirred. In this way they circled the room several times without anything decisive happening; indeed, because of its slow pace, the whole thing did not even look like a pursuit. That was why Gregor stayed on the floor for the time being, especially as he feared his father might regard an escape onto the walls or the ceiling as a particular piece of malice. Gregor had to admit to himself, however, that he would not be able to keep up even this running for long, for while his father took a single step, he had to carry out a countless number of movements. Shortness of breath was already beginning to make itself felt, since even in his former days his lungs had never been entirely trustworthy. As he staggered along in this way, gathering all his strength for running, barely keeping his eyes open; in his dullness not thinking of any other rescue than running; and having almost forgotten that the walls were open to him, though here, admittedly, they were blocked by carefully carved furniture full of notches and points — something, lightly tossed, came flying down right next to him and rolled on ahead of him. It was an apple; a second one flew after it at once; Gregor stopped dead in terror; there was no use running any farther, for his father had resolved to bombard him. He had filled his pockets from the fruit bowl on the sideboard and was now throwing apple after apple, for the moment without taking careful aim. These small red apples rolled about the floor as if electrified, knocking into one another. One weakly thrown apple grazed Gregor's back but glanced off harmlessly. But one that flew right after it actually lodged itself in Gregor's back; Gregor tried to drag himself onward, as if the startling, unbelievable pain might pass if he changed his position; but he felt as if nailed to the spot and stretched himself out, all his senses in utter confusion. With his last look he could still see the door of his room being flung open and his mother rushing out ahead of his screaming sister, in her chemise, for his sister had undressed her so that she could breathe freely in her faint; saw how his mother then ran toward his father, her loosened petticoats slipping to the floor one after the other as she went, and how, tripping over the petticoats, she pressed herself upon his father and, embracing him, in complete union with him — but now Gregor's sight was already failing — with her hands clasped behind his father's head, begged him to spare Gregor's life.

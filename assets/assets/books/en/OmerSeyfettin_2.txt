The Bomb
Ömer Seyfettin

In this ground-floor room, whose walls and ceiling had been blackened by the soot of a long winter, the squat, ugly hearth, standing there like a prisoner, seemed to burn the logs inside it in a fury, as if trying to devour them as fast as it could. The flames, catching quickly, stretching up and dying down, washed in a light, flowing red young Boris, who was playing a stirring socialist march on his mandolin, and his beautiful wife Magda, who sat facing him knitting her eternal, never-ending national stocking, and they set trembling the great, crippled shadows of the two that covered the whole room. Outside was a wild, cold February night. A raging wind beat against the closed shutters of the little window. Around the big, coarse, thick-legged table in the middle of the room stood chairs just as coarse and shapeless, dozing as if they were listening to the sharp voice of the mandolin. The dilapidated old clock above the hearth showed that midnight had passed. Boris leaned his mandolin against the wall. He stood up. All at once he stretched, his shadow spreading across the ceiling. Magda raised her lovely eyes from the tips of her quick needles:

"Are you sleepy?"

Young, strapping Boris answered, still stretching:

"No, I'm not sleepy at all..."

And, sitting down again, he added:

"I don't know why, but there's an uneasiness in me tonight."

Magda stopped short. She lowered her stocking onto her knees. She looked at her husband with hesitant, suspicious Slavic eyes. Knitting her brows, she muttered:

"There's an uneasiness in me too!"

Boris was only twenty-five years old. He was the one and only son of Baba İstoyan. After studying in his village under the teacher who had come from Sofia, he had gone to Sofia himself through the good offices of the young priest and finished his education there. Returning at the end of five years without the baggy breeches and sash of a peasant, vigorous and handsome, Boris had not stayed long in his father's house at his old mother's side; one day he had taken to the mountains. Only a few times a year would he come by night, see his mother and father, and vanish again. When the Young Turks proclaimed the Constitution that no one had expected, he, like all his comrades, had come down to the town, surrendered his weapon to the government and returned to his village. But his mother was no more. She had died three months before, crying "Ah, Boris! Ah, Boris!" to the very end. The neighbors said that her eyes had stayed open, and that although the priest had tried to close them with his hand, he had not been able to. After five years spent in desolate forests and fearful crags, always in the darkness of moonless nights, he found the free and easy, bright and green village very much to his liking. His father was very old by now. He could no longer manage the fields and the plowing; his hired men were cheating him. Boris took matters into his own hands. He had, in any case, a deep love of work. One day he ran into the schoolmistress of the village school. He knew her. This girl, too, had studied in Sofia, as he had. They talked, and before long they were in love. They married. After marrying Boris, Magda had given up the school and devoted her life to her home and her husband. Their love grew day by day, and the harmony of their ideas bound them to each other with an ever more ardent longing. And so for months now they would sit up past midnight like this, talking and loving each other, with no wish to sleep.

*****

Boris took up his mandolin again. He began to play. Magda knitted her stocking and thought. Three months from now, when their child was born, who knew how happy they would be! Lifting her eyes from the needles, she looked at her swollen belly. Beneath her skirt, in her womb, within her very self, lay Boris's little one. Touching her elbows to this swelling, she trembled with happiness and, glancing furtively at Boris as though she were doing something shameful, she blushed. The logs in the hearth crackled and fell in; all at once the flames multiplied, and it seemed the whole room filled with red shadows. Boris was playing the socialist march again. He finished. He looked at his wife with his blue eyes and repeated the refrain of the march:

"Da jivee truda!"

Magda raised her lovely head with its thick hair. She had fine eyebrows, a well-shaped nose and a fresh, rosy complexion. Her broad shoulders and full breasts seemed about to spill out of her dress. Below the sturdy legs under her skirts, lit by the flames, her feet looked very small and dainty. Pregnancy had made her even more beautiful, a more exquisite and perfect woman. Young, strapping Boris gazed at his wife with longing eyes, trying to throw off the uneasiness in his soul and forget this nameless sorrow. He wanted to talk:

"Long live labor and toil!" he said. "Isn't that so, Magda darling? Whoever works will be happy. I wonder when the socialists' dream will come true!"

Magda shook her head and smiled:

"Never, never, not ever... Their dream will always remain a dream! People will still be slaves in the hands of the wicked, and a great many men will deny the virtue of work."

Boris set down the mandolin from his lap. He put his hands in his trouser pockets. He stretched out his legs:

"Yes, I don't believe this dream will come true either," he said. "But I'm in love with the idea of humanity in it! Think of it. Wars will be done away with. There will be no more murders. Treacherous politics will be forgotten, and everyone like brothers... They'll work and they'll be happy..."

"Alas..."

Boris repeated it too:

"Alas from me as well! Especially here, in this filthy, savage place, in this cruel Macedonia, it's impossible to work in peace. Ah, this bloody Macedonia..."

Magda suddenly felt a pang just below her throat. She put down her stocking. She looked into Boris's face. Her heart had begun to beat fast. She heard the violent roar of the wind, like a curse. She heard the feeble, mournful ticking of the clock above the hearth, beating like a broken heart. She stood up. She threw her arms around Boris's neck:

"Ah, let's go to bed," she said. "Don't talk of such bitter things..."

Boris put his arm around Magda's waist, over her hip. He drew her onto his lap and sat her there. He kissed her on the lips. For a moment, a minute, a long minute they stayed like that. With his other hand Boris took hold of his wife's full breast. Slowly, in waves, he squeezed that soft, exquisite fullness that filled his whole palm. The eyes of both grew narrow, and they trembled. Magda's head sank against Boris's powerful chest. Boris laid his lips among that thick chestnut hair:

"Rest easy, Magda darling, you'll never be afraid again; you're going to be happy!"

The young woman wound her big bare legs around her husband's strong legs. She squeezed. Her whole body tensed, and she asked:

"Ah, are we going to be happy? How? Tell me, how?"

Boris was happy and delighted.

"Let me explain — believe me," he said. "We're leaving this place."

"Are we going to run away?"

"No, we're going to emigrate."

"Where?"

"To America..."

"To America?"

"Yes, to America, Magda darling. I've been getting ready for two months now. I didn't tell you. I was going to tell you on the very day we left. I had my father sell everything we own. Now we have eight hundred liras. With eight hundred liras a man who works can be very happy in America."

Magda squeezed her husband's legs even harder and, turning, threw her arms around his neck.

"Why did you hide it from me?" she said. "Oh, how happy we're going to be."

Kissing his wife's lips, Boris answered:

"There was a reason. You would have been frightened. I had to keep it from you."

"Tell me, what was it? What would I have been frightened of?"

"If I told you now, you'd be frightened all the same."

"Tell me, I won't be frightened."

Boris told her, hesitantly:

"Two months ago, one Sunday night, I was coming home. I found a letter at the door. I opened it. It had been written by the revolutionary committee."

"Ah..."

"Yes, by the revolutionary committee... It began, 'O godless Boris!' And it went on: 'You have begun to teach here the socialism you once wanted to spread in the mountains. You are a traitor! You are the enemy of our fatherland! Know that we shall hack that traitor's head of yours from your body with an ax and hand it over to those who follow you, to those who love you.'"

"Ah..."

"'Or else be our guide. Come up into the Balkan mountains with us... Work for the greater fatherland,' it said. That was when I understood that it would be impossible for me to live here with you, with your love. I talked my father round at once. I had him sell everything."

Kissing Boris again, Magda asked:

"Ah, when are we leaving? Tell me, when are we leaving this place?"

"So soon that... If I told you, you wouldn't believe me..."

"Tell me..."

"Tomorrow!"

"Tomorrow? Dear God..."

She sprang up from her husband's lap. She fluttered with joy. Then, sitting on her husband's knees again, she began to kiss him:

"Tomorrow, tomorrow... So this is our last night of sorrow..."

Pleased and delighted by his wife's joy, Boris went on, caressing her, amid kisses:

"Yes, Magda darling. Tomorrow we're going to America, and there we'll work. We'll have a small, comfortable, peaceful home. No committees, no brigands, no savagery, no murder! We'll only work. Our child will be born there, just after we arrive. My poor father will see there the reward for the seventy years of torment he has lived through. He'll die in his bed with an easy heart. At night, far from the fear of raids and of having our throats cut, we'll talk sweetly together. We'll feel love, life, beauty, goodness, virtue."

Magda was trembling:

"Oh, what happiness!"

Boris went on, still amid kisses:

"Then you'll see how sweet humanity can be! Beautiful, orderly cities... Theaters! Broad, bright streets, villages like paradise. People who know how to respect one another... Great charitable institutions that permit no misery... Hospitals, sanatoriums... Schools, universities... In short, paradise! It's impossible — you can't imagine these things."

Trembling even more, pressing her swollen belly with her elbows and her husband's legs with her own, Magda said:

"Oh, I can imagine them!"

Boris went on:

"Then on certain nights, in our tiny little house, with our child playing beside us, secure in our labor and our honor, we'll suddenly remember Macedonia, this land of cannibals. Before our eyes will rise filthy, narrow streets, wretched and naked men... Endless snows stained with blood, black, loathsome, sharp axes, and then the black, terrible Balkan mountains, Pirin! Our flesh will creep, and you'll run into my arms again, just like this. With my kisses I'll wipe the nightmares of this filthy, terrible Macedonia from your eyes..."

Magda felt a sweet faintness from contentment and happiness. Her full legs, which had been squeezing her husband's, went slack. Her arms dropped to her sides. Her eyes were gazing at distant dreams. Boris squeezed her in his arms again. He began kissing her on the lips:

"You see, here every minute of ours passes in grief, mourning and anguish. Not for one minute is our heart at ease. The slightest noise frightens us. Take now, for instance: even though I love you so, though I adore you so, there's no quiet corner left for my love in my mind, which is filled with dread. I can't fully taste the sweetness of your lips. There are such ugly, bloody pictures in my imagination... In short, here we're like poor, senseless, witless wild animals, terrified in the face of an unknown danger that is always with us."

The logs in the hearth had fallen in and the flames had died. Only the little lamp above the hearth shed a yellow light. Boris and Magda again embraced each other with all their strength. They kissed.

Boris spoke:

"From now on I'll truly feel love," he was saying. "In fact, I feel it already. I imagine myself in America; I picture us alone in our room, safe and at peace."

The kisses died on Magda's lips. Suddenly she said:

"Oh, I'm afraid!"

Boris asked in astonishment:

"Why?"

"I don't know. But I'm so afraid that..."

The young woman had been seized by a nervous agitation. She was truly trembling with fear and crying. Boris wanted to soothe this nameless, sudden grief:

"Don't be afraid, come, get up," he said. "Let's go to bed; let's live now the happiness that is so close at hand. Don't look at this filthy room, this filthy hearth, this nest of darkness and dread; close your eyes and imagine America. Come, get up, don't be afraid, dry your eyes!"

"I'm afraid, Boris, I'm afraid."

"Why, my love? Calm yourself. Our happiness has upset you; come, let's go to bed. Beside me, in my arms... There's nothing to be afraid of..."

They both got up. Boris threw his arm around Magda's neck. Magda clung to her husband's waist with a nervous longing. They were walking. They would go through one of the two doors that faced each other and go to bed. Magda stopped again:

"I'm afraid, Boris; listen, the dogs are barking..."

Boris answered:

"They always bark."

"No, they're running as they bark. Someone's coming!"

Hesitant and puzzled, Boris stopped. He twisted his lips:

"Who would come? It's long past midnight," he said.

The barking of the dogs, mingling with the wind, grew fiercer and came closer. They both stood stock-still. Boris's arm fell from Magda's shoulder. Magda drew her hand from Boris's waist. The dogs were running and barking as they went for a stranger. Boris said:

"Who is it? Let's look out of the window!"

He walked toward the window. Magda ran and put out the lamp so that no one could see inside. A thick, pitch-black darkness filled the room. The embers in the hearth began to glow like the tongue and bloody teeth of a fiend of hell. A fierce wind came in through the opened window, fanning these teeth of fire and making that black mouth utter invisible threats.

Boris shouted:

"Who's there?"

A distant, thin voice answered:

"Me!"

"Who are you?"

"Me, Melina..."

It was the neighbors' daughter. Why could she have come now? What was she looking for? Boris turned his face back into the room and looked for Magda, but he could not see her. Only the embers in the hearth caught his eye. It was so dark...

He looked out again into the darkness:

"What do you want?"

"Baba İstoyan?"

By now the girl had come close and was standing right under the window.

Boris turned to Magda:

"Light the lamp!" he said.

Once the room was lit, they looked into each other's faces. Boris asked:

"What could she want with my father?"

"I don't know; we'll find out in a moment."

And Magda suddenly walked to the door. She opened it. Along with the dark wind, a young girl came in. She was chilled through, and her cheeks had turned bright red. She had tucked her hands under her apron. Her chest was hollow, she was knock-kneed, and she was a little hunchbacked. Magda began to question this simple-minded girl, whom she had taught for a year:

"What are you going to do with Baba İstoyan?"

Melina gave no answer. First she said:

"Dobra vecher!"

Then she gaped stupidly around her:

"I'm not going to do anything!"

"Well then, what are you looking for?"

"I'm not the one who's looking."

"Who is, then?"

"The komitadjis..."

Magda suddenly went deathly pale. She leaned against the wall so as not to fall. She put her hand over her heart:

"The komitadjis?"

Wanting to leave, Melina said:

"I don't know... armed men, that's all..."

In a hopeless voice that could scarcely be heard, Magda asked again:

"From our village?"

The simple-minded girl explained:

"No. Men in yellow clothes, with rifles! They want Baba İstoyan, for sure. They're saying, 'If he doesn't come, we'll go over there, and we'll cut them all down and burn their house as well.'"

Magda's knees gave way. She held on to the wall with her hands; her jaw locked. She looked at Boris with a dead, empty gaze. He, too, stood pale and lost in thought. He took his right hand out of his trouser pocket. And he began to scratch his long hair. His eyes were on his feet. Without looking at the girl, he asked:

"Where are these men?"

"At our house... They're drinking wine."

"How many?"

"Four, counting the schoolmaster..."

Suddenly he raised his eyes to the girl:

"Go on, go and tell them I'll come..." he said. "Say, 'Baba İstoyan is ill.'"

The girl said "Sas zdrave" and went out. She had left the door open. A fierce wind came in, setting the lamplight wavering and stirring Magda's skirts. Boris looked at his wife. She was done for. It was as if she were about to die that very minute. She was deathly pale. He walked over and, with his strong arms, drew her away from the wall she was leaning on.

"Don't be afraid," he said. "I'll be back in half an hour."

Magda began to cry. Shaken by sobs that rose from deep within her, she begged:

"Ah, don't go, don't go, my Boris..."

Boris kissed her and tried to console and calm her. He explained that if he did not go, they would certainly come and commit some outrage, and that if Baba İstoyan went, they would demand money from the poor old man with insults and torture; and he added:

"The traitors have heard that my father sold everything. They've guessed that we mean to emigrate. Now they'll demand the eight hundred liras, all the fortune we have. If my father goes, they'll torture and humiliate him. So I'll go now. I'll talk with them and tell them I'm willing to go up into the mountains with them. And I'll tell them the money hasn't been paid to us yet, that it'll be in our hands in a week, and I'll talk them round. Tomorrow evening they won't find us here..."

Magda was despairing and distraught.

"Ah, they won't believe you; they'll do you some harm," she said.

Boris reassured her once more. At great length he explained that he would surely talk them round, that there was no other way but to act like this, and that if Baba İstoyan went, that was when the real harm would be done. Magda wept with deep, nervous sobs, writhing and clutching her chestnut hair with her slender fingers. Boris put his fur cap on his head. He kissed his wife again and pressed her hand.

"Come now, my love, be brave," he said. "I'll be back in half an hour and we'll go to bed. The excitements of this last night will be a memory for us."

He walked to the door. Magda walked with him. In a trembling voice she said:

"Let me come too, Boris!"

But her husband would not agree:

"What would you do there, among drunkards? Stay here and wait — half an hour, my love, be brave..."

"Ah, at least take your revolver with you..."

"I'm not going to war... I'm going to talk; there's no need for it!"

With that he went out in nervous haste. Magda was left standing at the door. The darkness was so black and dense that it could be grasped and felt. Her husband had vanished into this darkness. The terrifying darkness of this black, windy night that had swallowed her beloved Boris — a darkness that called nothingness to mind and gave warning of death — entered through her eyes into her body and her veins, mingled with her blood and seeped into her soul. Her head spun. She choked amid sobs and tears. She collapsed where she stood. Her eyes fixed on the wild black night, she wept under the black, cold wind, writhing with ceaseless sobs.

*****

Baba İstoyan always went to bed an hour after sunset, fell asleep at once, and woke two hours before daybreak. Winding his sash around him, he opened his door. When he saw his daughter-in-law stretched out on the threshold of the door to the yard, he was taken aback and said:

"What are you doing there?"

The moment Magda heard her father-in-law's voice, she got up. She pulled herself together:

"Nothing!" she answered. "I was going to step outside, and I lay down for a moment."

The old man walked toward the hearth. Magda pushed the door shut. And she ran to the hearth. She threw on logs and began to kindle them. The old man was filling his pipe. After lighting the fire, Magda sat down on a chair beside the coarse, thick-legged table in the middle of the room. She propped her elbows on it. She took her head in her hands. It had now been half an hour. Boris had not come back yet. Why was he so late? Her heart was wrung, and she wanted to cry out at the top of her voice and weep, to throw herself on the floor, to run to Melina's house, find Boris and throw herself into his arms. Baba İstoyan, hunched over, was drawing on his pipe, calm and sullen, and picking his nose with the middle finger of his left hand. The dilapidated old clock, as though it had stopped and then started running again of its own accord, once more made its mournful ticking heard; the burning logs again filled the room with red shadows and red phantoms; the cold wind howled more savagely, more treacherously, and shook the window shutter all the harder. Magda was thinking of Boris and sobbing in secret. Her heart swelled until it made her chest ache. From outside came the sound of distant barking. Those must be the neighbors' dogs. Suddenly she raised her head. Boris must have left by now. The dogs were barking. She listened. Oh, Boris was coming. For a few minutes she did not move; she stayed just as she was.

Now the barking was coming closer. Their own dogs were barking too. But why? Why were they barking at Boris? Could there be a stranger with him? The barking came closer still. Magda stood up. She went to the window. She did not dare to open the shutter; a nameless fear held her motionless... The dogs had come very close. Footsteps could be heard. Magda's heart stopped. Her breath failed her. She lost all sense of herself.

Suddenly there was a knock at the door. Baba İstoyan jumped and dropped his pipe. Magda put her hand over her heart. She hunched her shoulders. She drew in her neck. The door was knocked again, harder. Baba İstoyan got up. He walked toward the window. In a feeble voice he said:

"Who's there?"

A tuneless voice answered from outside:

"Open up, Baba İstoyan, it's us. We've come to talk. Don't be afraid!"

Hesitant and fearful, the old man asked again:

"Who are you?"

"Captain Raçof, Pançe, Sandre..."

The old man froze where he stood, as if struck by lightning. These were the most terrible, the bloodiest, the most merciless, the cruelest of the komitadjis. Their names made every village on the plain tremble. Like a ghost, the old man walked to the door. He opened it. A tall man in brown clothes appeared, a Mannlicher on his shoulder. His eyes were small and bloodshot. He had a scrawny and exceedingly ugly neck. With that servile, downtrodden manner peculiar to Bulgarian peasants alone, Baba İstoyan bowed, and with that crooked, false salute that is likewise peculiar to these peasants, he greeted the voivode:

"Come in, Gospodin!"

Behind Raçof were two more men. They too were armed with Mannlicher rifles. Cartridge belts crossed over their waists and chests. One was short and dark. The other was fair like Raçof, but younger and less ugly. Magda's eyes had opened wide and her face had gone white as a sheet. She ran. She clasped Raçof's feet. She began to kiss them, pleading as she wept:

"Gospodin! Where is Boris? Ah, where is Boris?"

Raçof stroked the lovely hair of the lovely woman who had thrown herself at his feet with fingers that looked like swollen, mud-caked snakes.

"Get up, socialist schoolmistress," he said. "Your Boris will be here presently. Now let's talk our business..."

And he walked on. Magda was left on the floor. He sat down without ceremony on the chair beside the table. He laid his rifle on the table. The others sat down facing him. The short, dark one held a round thing wrapped in a black cloth. They too laid their rifles on the table, and the round thing wrapped in black cloth was set down beside the rifles. Raçof turned to Baba İstoyan:

"Come along then, squire," he said, "sit across from me. I'm going to talk with you. Magda, you come over here beside me too. If your father gets stubborn, give us a hand, so we won't have to do anything nasty."

Neither of them hesitated. Baba İstoyan sat down across from Raçof. Magda beside him... Baba İstoyan was completely dazed. He could not make out why his daughter-in-law was asking the komitadjis where his son was. Wasn't Boris at home?

Raçof took a tobacco case from his pocket. He put it down in the middle. He rolled a cigarette. With a quick movement Magda got up and took an ember from the hearth. She lit the bandit's cigarette. Then she threw the ember back into the hearth. She sat down again in the place she had risen from. Raçof took a few puffs on his cigarette. And, watching the smoke, he began:

"Well, Baba İstoyan, first of all promise us you won't make too much trouble! To cut a long story short, let's not waste time... Bring out the eight hundred liras."

The old man shuddered. The fruit, the sum, the whole total of possessions earned over sixty years with so much thrift and hardship... was being demanded now, at a moment like this. He was going to go mad. He denied it:

"What eight hundred liras?"

The voivode smiled. His dirty, broken teeth showed. He drew on his cigarette again. He nodded:

"I see. So you're going to make trouble. You surely won't talk until you've been beaten, until your feet are burned, until your nails are pulled out! And if you still won't talk, your son Boris is our prisoner. We'll cut his throat. We'll burn your house down too. And we still won't leave you in peace."

Baba İstoyan was staring down in front of him. They had even sold the house they were living in. If he gave up these eight hundred liras, he would surely die of hunger. He had not so much as a donkey left. When Magda heard them speak of cutting Boris's throat, she began to cry. She threw herself again at Captain Raçof's feet:

"Mercy, Gospodin, spare Boris..."

Raçof answered with a laugh:

"Pretty schoolmistress! You too have been faithless to the cause. Instead of both of you working for us here, you made your father sell his property so you could run away. You wanted to steal the money the nation spent on you. Well, that we won't allow. We're taking the money out of your hands. You won't be going anywhere from here. You'll work for us."

And he added:

"Come on, if you love your Boris, bring the money. If Baba İstoyan is stubborn, your darling's head will be cut off. You'll never see him again as long as you live."

When Magda thought that Boris might be killed, she almost went mad. She got up and, weeping, threw her arms around Baba İstoyan:

"Give it to them, father dear, give it to them; we're young. Boris and I will work and earn it again. Tell me where it is; I'll go and fetch it."

Raçof and his comrades watched Magda's pleading and laughed among themselves. Baba İstoyan was utterly stupefied. It was as if he did not hear the words at all and did not understand their meaning. For the miserly peasant, dying was far easier than handing over this money. Magda kept pleading. Suddenly Baba İstoyan raised his head. He said to Raçof:

"Captain, at least leave me a hundred liras of it to buy a house. So that I won't be left without a roof at the end of my days."

Raçof refused:

"No, I won't leave you a hundred. Your son is young; he'll work. He'll feed you. Come on, bring it, I tell you. Time is passing."

The old man hesitated; Magda pleaded. Raçof made a sign. The short, dark one picked up his rifle. He brought the butt down on the old man's back in a terrible blow. Raçof stood up. He asked fiercely:

"Well, Baba İstoyan, we're going to start the beating. We'll put your feet in the fire. Time is passing. Are you going to bring the money?"

Magda, in tears, was struggling and clinging to the old man. The old man said nothing. He nodded. He went to the door of the room where he slept. He went in. A minute later he came back with a heavy red bundle. He set it down on the table. The bandits were glad to have got the money so quickly. Raçof at once untied the bundle. He began to count.

"Well done, Baba İstoyan," he kept saying. "You gave us no trouble. Now bring us some wine; let's have some fun..."

The counting of the money was finished. Raçof divided the liras into three. He and his comrades put them into their bags. Then they shouted:

"Where's the wine? Where's the wine?"

Magda got to her feet. She ran to the little door of the storeroom. She opened it and went in. She brought out three glasses and a jug of wine. She set them before the bandits. Then she went into the storeroom again. She brought out peppers and pickles to go with the drink. The komitadjis were drinking hastily, one glass after another. Raçof spoke up:

"No need for morsels like these. We don't care for lifeless morsels..."

He seized Magda, who understood nothing of these words, by the waist and tried to kiss her. Magda resisted and began to cry. Not letting go of the young woman, Raçof was saying:

"I'll take my morsel from your cheeks. Aren't you a socialist? Socialists want a share in everything. So I'm sharing your cheeks with Boris!"

Magda was struggling. In his ugly, grating voice Raçof said:

"If you carry on with this nonsense, you won't see your Boris. We'll cut his throat."

When Magda heard this, she began to sob again and plead with Raçof. Now Raçof was kissing her fresh cheeks to his heart's content. He drank glass after glass and kissed her again and again. To his comrades he said:

"You take your morsel too, lads!"

He flung Magda into their arms like a lifeless ball of yarn. These two strong fellows fastened onto this exquisite woman. One of them was trying to lift her skirts. The other was even worse for drink. With his teeth he had bitten the cheek of the lovely head he held in his hand. Magda suddenly screamed. And she tore herself free of their arms. Blood was running from two places on her right cheek. So as not to see this scene, Baba İstoyan crouched at the edge of the hearth and took his head in his hands. He fixed his eyes on the fire. Magda had put her hand to her cheek. Blood oozed freely between her fingers, and she wept. The bandits seemed to take pleasure in watching this beautiful woman weep in her own blood. They were all silent. The old clock, as if moved by this assault and outrage, once more made its ticking heard, and the crowing of roosters mingled with the noise of the wind. Raçof pretended to feel sorry:

"Don't cry, Magda," he said. "Your Boris will be here presently. He'll kiss it better. The pain will go away. Come, I'll play the mandolin; dance for us a little..."

And, taking the mandolin from beside the hearth, he began to play a polka. Magda, weeping, kept saying:

"I don't know how to dance, Captain!"

Raçof got up. He went over to Magda. He said into her ear, in a forceful, savage voice:

"If you don't dance, if you spoil our fun, you won't see Boris; we'll go and cut his throat..."

Magda's whole body shook. The tears in her eyes stopped. And in a resigned voice she said:

"I'll dance... ah, Boris..."

Raçof sat down. He began to play the mandolin. The other two bandits drank without stopping and, laughing, watched Magda dance. The blood running from her cheek flowed down her white neck, giving her the look of a martyr come back to life. The bandit who had bitten her voiced another wish:

"Captain," he said, "let her lift her skirts and dance like that. Let's see her legs..."

Raçof turned to Magda:

"Come on, Magda, do this too! Let them see your legs! Then we'll be off and send your Boris back..."

The young woman stopped for a moment. She looked at Baba İstoyan. He had his face turned to the fire and did not see them at all. So these fellows were now defiling her honor as well. But her Boris was in danger. If she did not do what they wanted, her Boris, whom she loved so much, would have his throat cut. Never again would she see his thick chestnut hair, his blue eyes, his small red lips, his sweet smile. She closed her eyes and lifted her skirts. Keeping her feet in time with the polka that was playing, she began to leap. The bandits went wild. The captain began to play more fiercely and eagerly. The others could not stay in their seats; gazing at those white, full legs and at the lustful, arousing movements of their leaps, they threw their arms around each other's necks, pushing and shoving. They got up and went over to Raçof. They whispered something in his ear. Raçof said:

"All right, but it's too late! It's getting light! We've stayed too long!"

Then he looked at the beautiful woman with a deep, bestial, stirring greed and lamented:

"Ah, if only there were time!"

They got up. They slung their rifles over their shoulders. They were drunk. Their steps were tangling. Raçof said:

"God keep you, Baba İstoyan!"

The old peasant might have been dead. He gave no answer at all. Magda threw herself at the bandit's feet again:

"Please, Captain, send Boris back. You've taken everything we had. If he doesn't come, we'll die of hunger and despair. Pity us. Have mercy on us..."

Raçof laughed:

"He'll come, he'll surely come, surely... If you want him to come sooner, give me a kiss from that bloody cheek..."

Magda drew back in fury. The bandit caught hold of the woman again. He kissed her bloody cheek by force. He licked it. They were going out. The short, dark one remembered the thing wrapped in black cloth that they had brought with them.

"Captain," he said, "what are we going to do with the bomb?"

Raçof thought for a moment. He turned back:

"Magda, look at me!"

Magda trembled, thinking she was about to suffer yet another outrage. But this bandit was courteous and gallant.

"Do you see this, Magda? This thing here is a bomb. If you had made trouble and not handed over the money, we would have taken it from you by force all the same, and as punishment we would have tied the two of you together and blown you up with this bomb. But you were sensible. You gave us no trouble. There's no need for punishment. Now I have a favor to ask of you: you're going to keep this bomb for me. Mind you don't go handing it over to the gendarmes or anyone. Well, do you promise? And I'll go and let Boris go at once..."

Magda answered with hope and eagerness:

"I promise, Gospodin! For the love of God, send Boris at once."

Raçof asked again:

"I'll send him, but will you keep this bomb faithfully?"

"I will."

"Where?"

"In my own dowry chest. In the most secret, most treasured place!"

"Bravo! I'm pleased. Well then, God keep you!"

They all shook the beautiful woman's hand hard and went out the door. Outside it was growing faintly light, paling into gray. The dogs began to bark. The men were walking among the distant buildings, which stood like dense shadows, and singing a song. Ah, now Boris would come. The young woman gazed at the night as it began to lift, and heard all the roosters in the village crowing as though answering one another. Her heart pounding, she waited for her Boris. One of the departing bandits shouted. It was like the threat of an ominous, phantom nightmare:

"Hey, Magda, watch out, your bomb's going to go off."

The young woman pricked up her ears. The threat came again, as if echoing from unknown, savage chasms, from the voids of nothingness:

"Hey, Magda, watch out, your bomb's going to go off."

One would have thought that the roosters crowing everywhere had stilled the wind. In the distance, above the haylofts, a false dawn was opening its violet eyes. The young woman brooded, without really knowing what she thought. These bandits could do anything, savageries beyond imagining. Perhaps they had set the time fuse of this bomb so that it would go off, and left it like that. Now it would suddenly explode, and when her poor dear Boris came, he would find nothing but a ruined house and unrecognizable bloody scraps of flesh and bone. With mechanical haste she rushed to pick up this plaything of disaster. It was right in the middle of the table. She reached out. She lifted it. With her other hand she had held it from underneath. She felt a lukewarm wetness. She looked at her hand: it was bloody. Blood?... Then she set this dangerous bomb, which was not as heavy as she had imagined, down in front of her. She wanted to see its dial, its fuse. Slowly she untied the black cloth. There was a second cloth. This cloth was bright red with blood. She untied that cloth too. Chestnut hair came into view. She looked, she looked, she looked closely...

And suddenly she let out such a terrible, such a piercing, such a harrowing, such a horrifying scream that Baba İstoyan, by the hearth, leapt up and ran to his daughter-in-law. The poor woman's eyes had started from their sockets, her tangled hair stood straight on end, her shoulders were rigid, and she was staring with awe and horror at something she held in both hands. He looked closely. The thing she held was the severed, bloody head of his son, of handsome, chestnut-haired Boris, torn from his body...

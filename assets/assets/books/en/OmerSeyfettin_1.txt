The Pink Pearl Caftan
Ömer Seyfettin

The Imperial Council, beneath its great cool dome, was quieter today, more full of shadow. The blue, violet and squirrel-grey spring light filtering through the windows gathered and deepened in the green depths of its tiles. The weary viziers, kneeling on high silk cushions, gazed at the colored patterns of the carpet before them, and the dim eyes of the old Grand Vizier, who held his long white beard in his frail hand, were lost in points that did not exist, as though he were pondering things very distant and very dark.

"We need a brave man, pashas..." he said. "The envoy he sent us, drowned in gold braid, gold and diamonds — we did not let him kiss our Sultan's hand; we allowed him only to kiss his knee. No doubt he, too, will try to pay us back in kind."

"No doubt."

"Without the slightest doubt."

"Certainly..."

Seeing that the viziers of the Dome were entirely of his opinion, the Grand Vizier spoke his mind more plainly:

"Then the man who goes as our envoy must be very brave! A man who does not fear death. Who will stand up to anything that touches the honor of the state. Who will not, for fear of death, bow his head to the insults he meets..."

"Yes!"

"By all means."

"Very true..."

The Grand Vizier drew his hand from his beard and rested it on his knee. He straightened. He raised his head. He looked at the viziers one by one, their gleaming plumes trembling:

"Come, then... Find a brave man," he said. "Among the senior scribes, the Inner Palace, the Council, I can think of no one so bold. You think on it too."

"..."

"..."

"..."

"..."

And the Council — the silent little brain of the vast empire of a pious, peace-loving, quiet Sultan — began to think.

*****

This envoy was to be sent to İsmail the Safavid, who seven years later, under the terrible blow of Destiny that bore the name "Yavuz," would pay in a single stroke for all his pride and all his crimes! Bayezid the Saint, who had spent his years as a prince with books rather than riding, playing at the javelin or handling weapons, was of an exceedingly mild nature. He loved only poetry, philosophy and mysticism; he loathed war and strife. His viziers held it their greatest duty not to disturb the peace of their beloved Sultan. Even so, there was no end to the fighting on the frontiers. Campaigns against Bosnia, Wallachia, Karaman, Belgrade, Transylvania, Croatia and Venice followed one upon another; Modon, Koron, Zonchio and Santa Maura were conquered. It was as though the resolve and genius of the Conqueror of İstanbul did not die out even in the reign of his ascetic successor — who, the moment he took the throne, had his father's statue broken because "its shadow falls upon the ground," hoping to earn merit in God's eyes — but lived on like an undying flame, an eternal spirit. The more peace was sought, the more trouble piled upon trouble. And the East above all... It writhed in blood, in fire, in tyranny. Upon the ruins of the fallen, extinguished Akkoyunlu dynasty, Shah İsmail had set up an upstart kingdom. This rabid Shah, who left not a tree standing wherever he passed, and who had been seized by a mad pride for having avenged his father and his grandfather Cüneyd, lashed out right and left with unimaginable savagery. This cruel Shah — who, at a banquet to which he had invited even those who had sought refuge with him, had them thrown into great boiling cauldrons and served up as cold meat, as though they were a dish, and who drank wine from the skull of the Uzbek sovereign he had defeated — was truly a tyrant without equal in the world. The cultivated, calm, gentle and devout viziers of Bayezid's Council could not bear even to recall his atrocities. One day this tyrant would surely violate our frontiers too, and try to seize the eastern provinces. Everyone knew it. The year before, he had asked Alâüddevle, the ruler of Zülkadriye, for his daughter's hand in marriage. Alâüddevle would not give her. Enraged by the insult of this refusal, İsmail crossed the Sultan's lands to take his revenge. He entered the defenseless territory of Zülkadriye and took the fortresses of Diyarbekir and Harput. Alâüddevle fled to a steep mountain, and his son and two grandsons fell into İsmail's hands as captives. Shah İsmail had these poor wretches roasted over the fire like kebab. He ate their flesh as if it were lamb. Such savagery had never before been heard of in the East. The Sultan, who wanted no war, did nothing more than send an army to Ankara under the command of Yahya Pasha. This Shah was as cunning as he was cruel. He apologized for having crossed Ottoman soil and sent envoy after envoy. Prince Yavuz, then governor of Trabzon, had not been able to show patience as his father did; he had crossed the Tabriz frontier, plundered everything as far as Bayburd and Erzincan, and had even taken the Shah's brother İbrahim prisoner. Now İsmail's envoy was complaining of this incursion as well, and repeating that their recent raids into Ottoman territory had been aimed not at the Sultan's state but solely at Alâüddevle. And so the Council could find no suitable envoy to send to this cunning, cruel, ruthless upstart; for this rogue, who held himself the equal of the Ottoman Khan and was even carving out a world empire across the whole East, would surely commit all manner of outrages against the man who stood before him in the name of the state; and whoever answered those outrages he might well impale, or flay alive, or put to death with some crude, unimaginable savagery. At the Grand Vizier's right, the red-plumed turban that had sat motionless as a tombstone all this while stirred. Slowly it turned to the left:

"I know a man who is just right for this embassy," said its wearer. "His father was my comrade. But he will not accept government office."

"Who?"

"Muhsin Çelebi."

The Grand Vizier did not know this man. He asked:

"Does he live here?"

"Yes."

"What does he do?"

"He is fairly wealthy. He spends his time reading. You would not know him, my lord. He never keeps company with the great. He does not seek advancement."

"Why not?"

"I couldn't say — perhaps because 'every rise has its fall.'"

"Strange..."

"But he is very brave. He never strays from the truth. He does not shrink from death. He has fought for the faith many times. His face bears sword scars."

"Would he not serve as our envoy?"

"I don't know."

"If we could just see him once..."

"I don't know whether he would come to you even if you summoned him."

"How could he not come?"

"He just might not... He owes the world nothing. In his eyes a shah and a beggar are one and the same."

"Does he not love his state?"

"I believe he does."

"Then we shall summon him not for our own sake, but to serve his state."

"Do try, my lord."

......

That evening the Grand Vizier sent his steward to Muhsin Çelebi's house in Üsküdar. He wrote that he wished to speak with him on a matter concerning the state and the nation, and that he must come tomorrow without fail and without hesitation.

*****

After the morning prayer, as the Grand Vizier sat in the men's quarters of his palace, in a small, dim room hung with heavy curtains of Indian cloth, reading the papers his secretary had left, they announced that Muhsin Çelebi had arrived.

"Bring him here," he said.

Before two minutes had passed, a big, strapping, cheerful man with a sweeping scimitar mustache came in through the room's walnut door inlaid with mother-of-pearl. His large eyes shone beneath thin black brows. The weapon belt at his waist was empty. The Grand Vizier, accustomed to the groveling and prostrations of all his servants, waited for a moment for him to throw himself at the hem of his robe. The tassel on the heavy gold-thread fringe of the purple broadcloth divan on which he sat — a fringe forever being kissed — hung there bewildered, like a small, hollow head of gold. The Grand Vizier could find nothing to say. It was the first time in his life he had seen such a man, chest thrust out, head held high. Even the viziers of the Dome stood bent double in his presence. Muhsin Çelebi asked in a perfectly natural voice:

"I hear you sent for me. What is it you wish to tell me, sir?"

"Well..."

"Please, go on, sir."

"Come, my son, sit down here first..."

Without hesitation, without awkwardness, without cringing or shrinking, Muhsin Çelebi sat down with a perfectly natural movement on the cushion he was shown. Looking at the curled papers still in his hands, the Grand Vizier was saying to himself: "What sort of man is this? Could he be mad?" And yet... No. This gentleman was a thoroughly sensible man! He had wealth enough never to be beholden to anyone, worthy or unworthy. He ran the great dairy and the great farm behind the Çamlıca woods, lived honorably, and bowed to no one. He looked after the poor, the weak and the friendless; his table was never without guests. He was devout. But he was no fanatic. He was one of those who felt in their hearts the love of faith, nation and Sultan. He understood the greatness and sanctity of his state. His one ideal was "to bow down to no one but God, and never to be the slave of a slave"... His learning and accomplishment were known to all. When İbn-i Kemal spoke of him, he would say, "He could teach me!" He was a poet. Yet never in his life had he written a single panegyric ode. He would not even read such eulogies... He was past forty. He had not yet turned onto a single one of the roads to advancement that had opened before him. He knew that at the end of these radiant, paradise-like roads, paved with gold and flowered with enamel, there always stood "the altar of a dirty hem." In his eyes humanity was very high, very great. Man was God's vicegerent upon the earth. God had wished to give man His own character. Man stood above every created thing. Fawning suited a dog well enough, wagging its tail as it licked its master's shoes; but a man... Muhsin Çelebi loathed the greedy buffoons who swallowed every kind of humiliation and climbed bent double to the heights of fortune, the slaves without self-respect, the filthy captives who crawled on the ground like reptiles. Indeed, he had become a recluse just so as not to see them. Only in time of war did he come out into the open, to command the Guraba cavalry companies. His free and natural way of sitting in the great man's presence astonished the Grand Vizier. But it did not anger him:

"We wish to send an envoy to Tabriz. Would you go on our behalf, my son?"

"I?"

"Yes."

"Why me, of all people?"

"Well, we cannot find the kind of man we are looking for..."

"I have never held any office of state until now."

"Why not?"

Muhsin Çelebi paused for a moment. He swallowed. He smiled:

"Because I do not bow my head, and I do not kiss hands and hems," he said. "Whereas the great men of our day have all risen to their posts by bowing their heads, by kissing hands and hems and even feet, with a thousand kinds of fawning, hypocrisy and groveling, and so they always gather about them men who repeat the ugly deeds of their own abject past. Their favorites, their boon companions, their protégés are all base hypocrites, unprincipled flatterers, dishonorable buffoons, spineless toadies. Let them see a man who is manly, honest, self-respecting and free, who listens to the voice of his conscience, and at once they bear him a grudge and work for his ruin. Why was Gedik Ahmed Pasha stabbed, my Pasha?"

"..."

The Grand Vizier slowly clenched his teeth. He narrowed his eyes. He crumpled the paper he was holding. He could not bring himself to anger. But a tremor came to his cheeks, as it did when he was angry. Not while he was vizier — not even back when he was only a governor-general — had any of his peers dared to speak to him so bluntly. Again he thought, "Could he be mad?" If he was not mad... what insolence this was! Was insolence of this degree not contrary to "the order of the world"? He narrowed his eyes still further. "What if I had this fellow's head cut off..." he said to himself. He was about to open his mouth to call for the guards. Suddenly he heard the deep voice of his conscience — rising from some unknown depth of it — say: "There — you too, like those who rise by the roads of fawning, hypocrisy and groveling, cannot bear a free and straight word! You too want before you not a manly human being but a dog that licks your feet, a buffoon bent double under his own abasement, a wretch!" He opened his narrowed eyes. He put down beside him the paper he had been squeezing in his palm. He looked at Muhsin Çelebi again. His high forehead, with the scar of a broad sword wound gleaming in its middle... his ruddy cheeks... his thick white neck, freshly shaved... his rather large, hooked nose... his fine turban... he looked exactly like the pictures of the heroes of old in the pages of the Shahnameh. Yes, this was a living hero, one whom the sword whose wound showed on his brow had failed to bring down. The fair-minded Grand Vizier did not smother the voice of conscience echoing in his soul with the darkness of his pride. "Here is exactly the man we are looking for..." he told himself. A man this fearless could not endure an insult to his state and his nation either; he could never, out of fear of death, meekly accept the insults he met. He nodded his great turban gently:

"We shall send you to Tabriz as our envoy."

Muhsin Çelebi asked:

"You have so many chancellors, secretaries and scholars about you. Why don't you choose one of them?"

"Do you know who this villain called Shah İsmail is?"

"I do."

"Do you love your state?"

"I do."

The wise Grand Vizier sat up. He leaned back:

"Very well, then," he said. "This villain does not accept the rule that 'no harm may come to an envoy.' He fancies himself our rival. What he cannot do to us on the field of battle, he will want to do to the envoy we send. He may well put him to death under torture. For he has no fear of God. And an insult to our envoy is an insult to our state. We need a man who, when insulted, will not fear for his head... who will pay that villain back in the very same coin... If you love your state, you will accept this sacrifice!"

Muhsin Çelebi did not stop to think:

"I accept, sir — but on one condition," he said.

"What condition?"

"Since this is a sacrifice, it cannot be done for pay. A sacrifice is made freely. A sacrifice made for the state in return for pay, whatever it may be, is in truth nothing but personal gain. I want no salary, no office, no fee of any kind. I will perform this service without reward. That is my condition!"

"But, my son, how can that be? His envoy was most richly dressed. His horses and his retinue were magnificent. Our envoy's horses, retinue and garments must be even more splendid, even richer. For these we shall certainly give you several thousand gold pieces from the treasury."

Muhsin Çelebi turned. He looked down. Then he raised his head:

"No," he said, "I will not take a single copper from the treasury. I shall fit out the magnificently harnessed horses and the finely dressed attendants that are needed with my own money. In fact..."

"..."

The Grand Vizier's eyes widened.

"...In fact, I shall wear on my back something so rich that Shah İsmail has never seen its like in all his life."

"What will you wear?"

"I shall buy the 'Pink Pearl Caftan' from Toroğlu the gold-embroiderer — the one whose brocade came from India and whose trimmings came from Venice."

"What... Where will you find that much money, my son?"

The Grand Vizier had every right to be astonished. There was no one in İstanbul who had not heard of this caftan, finished only a month before and embroidered all over with the rarest pink pearls. Each time viziers or ambassadors approached Toroğlu to buy it as a gift for the Sultan, he raised its price. Muhsin Çelebi explained how he would buy this famous caftan:

"I shall mortgage my farm, my dairy and my house, and borrow ten thousand gold pieces from the merchants. Two thousand I shall spend on the horses and attendants. With the remaining eight thousand I shall buy the caftan."

The Grand Vizier did not think this sensible:

"Once you are back, that caftan will be of no use to you. It is nothing but an instrument of pomp. You will lose your property. You will fall into poverty."

"No. The caftan I buy for eight thousand gold pieces, Toroğlu will buy back from me in six months for seven thousand. With seven thousand I shall redeem my farm. And if I cannot pay off the rest of my debts, then let the dairy my father left me be sacrificed for the state... One cannot always take from the state, after all... Sometimes one must give a little, too!"

"..."

The longer he talked with Muhsin Çelebi, the greater the Grand Vizier's amazement grew. His heart was eased. Here at last was the very man to send to put an insolent upstart ruler in his place. He laughed and nodded his heavy turban. The refined, timid, calculating gentlemen of the Council loved their lives and their property dearly. Had one of them been sent as envoy, he would have thought more of the reward he would receive than of the dignity of his state, and would have swallowed every insult dealt him. The Grand Vizier wanted to keep Muhsin Çelebi for dinner as well. He did not succeed, and when his guest left, he saw him out all the way to the hall.

*****

......Within six months Muhsin Çelebi had mortgaged his great farm, his dairy, his house, his shop, his garden and his vegetable plot. He raised money from the merchants. He fitted out his horses and his attendants. All of it was truly magnificent beyond compare. He also bought the famous Pink Pearl Caftan from Toroğlu, on condition that he would sell it back for seven thousand gold pieces on his return. He left his young wife and two small children at the house of a relative. He put six months' living money into their hands. Then, placing the Sultan's letter in his bosom, he set out on the road. As he advanced from stage to stage, the pomp and splendor of this new envoy, and above all the fame of his pearl caftan, spread across all of Anatolia and spilled over into the lands of Shah İsmail. One day Muhsin Çelebi entered the citadel of Tabriz in great magnificence. The people of this little capital, enamored as they were of finery, splendor, color and ornament, were dumbfounded when they saw the İstanbul envoy's caftan. The city, the palace, every gathering was full of the story of the caftan. Shah İsmail had heard of the "Pink Pearl" only in fairy tales and had never yet seen what such a thing looked like. He felt a deep grudge in his soul against this rich envoy who possessed something he himself had never seen. He resolved to crush him beneath humiliation. Before receiving him, he had his executioners stationed behind his throne. He had the brocade cushions and silk prayer rugs in front of the throne taken away. His viziers stood on his right, his warriors on his left.

...Muhsin Çelebi walked in with free strides through the open door beneath its wide porphyry arch. He advanced. His head was high as always, his chest thrust forward as always. He drew the imperial letter from his bosom and kissed it. He touched it to his head. Then he held it out to the Shah, who was perched on the golden throne with the strange stillness of a bird of prey — as if wrapped in heaps of silk, red, green, blue and purple, and bound up in gold thread, plumes and banners. The Shah, whose foot had not been kissed, turned pale with fury. The whites of his eyes vanished. He took the letter. When Muhsin Çelebi stepped back from before the throne, he glanced around him. There was nothing to sit on. He smiled. "It seems they mean to keep me standing, forced into a posture of reverence..." he said to himself. He thought for a moment. How should he answer this insult? At once he took the Pink Pearl Caftan from his back. He spread it on the floor before the throne. Shah İsmail, his viziers and his commanders were stupefied, staring in amazement. Then he sat down cross-legged on that precious caftan. And in a booming voice that set ringing the pointed dome, delicately painted with giants and dragons, and the gilded arches:

"The great Sultan whose letter I have delivered is of the line of Oğuz Kara Han!" he cried. "Since the world was created, not one of his forefathers has ever been any man's subject. Every one of them was a sultan, every one a khan. The envoy of a sovereign whose ancestors have been rulers since the Creation does not stand in humble attendance before any foreign sovereign. For there is no sovereign in the world as noble as his own."

For... As Muhsin Çelebi shouted his speech in plain, blunt Turkish, the Shah, who knew no Persian, turned red, then yellow, then purple, and the letter in his hand, which he was too agitated to open, shook and shook. The executioners behind his throne had drawn their swords. Muhsin Çelebi thundered on and on. The courtiers, viziers, executioners and warriors marveled at their ruler's patience and forbearance. Some of them even began to mutter. When he had finished speaking, Muhsin Çelebi asked for no leave or anything of the kind; he simply stood up. He walked toward the door. Shah İsmail sat frozen, turned to stone. The pride that was to be shattered at Çaldıran had melted today under the fiery gaze of this lone Turk. As Muhsin Çelebi was going out, the Shah said to his courtiers, who were as frozen with amazement as he was:

"Give that fellow his caftan," he said.

One of the warriors ran. He gathered up the caftan spread before the throne. He caught up with the Turkish envoy:

"Here, sir. You are forgetting your caftan."

Muhsin Çelebi stopped and laughed. Turning back toward the door he had just come through, he spoke in a voice loud enough for the Shah to hear:

"No, I am not forgetting it. I am leaving it to you. In your palace you have no prayer rug, no cushion on which to seat the envoy of a great sovereign... Besides, a Turk never puts back on his shoulders a thing he has once spread on the ground... Did you not know that?" he said.

*****

...He galloped back night and day along the roads he had come by. By the time he entered Üsküdar, Muhsin Çelebi had not a single coin left in his pocket. He said to his finely dressed attendants:

"My children! I give you the horses you ride, their saddlecloths and harness, the clothes on your backs, the jeweled daggers at your belts. Do you release me from any claim you have on me?"

"We do."

"We do."

"As freely as our mothers' milk."

Having received their answer, he sent them on their way. He drew a deep breath. Without stopping at his house, he hurried down to the shore. He jumped into a skiff. He went to the Grand Vizier's mansion. He said that he had delivered the letter to the Shah, that he had suffered no insult, and that without deigning to ask the Shah's leave he had risen and returned to İstanbul unannounced. The Grand Vizier had in any case been utterly certain that he would carry out his duty as it deserved. He asked a few questions about the roads, the local lords and the tribes. When the Çelebi rose to take his leave:

"I would like to buy it, my son. Is your caftan here?" he said.

"No, I did not bring it."

"Did you sell it in Persia?"

"No, I did not sell it."

"Was it stolen?"

"No."

"Then what did you do with it?"

"Nothing!"

The Grand Vizier insisted and asked again and again. But he could not find out what had become of the caftan. Muhsin Çelebi was not so small of soul as to boast of what he had done. That evening he returned to Üsküdar. Nor did he tell Toroğlu the gold-embroiderer, who sought him out the next day to buy the caftan back for seven thousand gold pieces, what he had done with it. In curious İstanbul no one ever learned "how, where or why" the famous Pink Pearl Caftan had been left behind. The adventure in the palace at Tabriz sank into the darkness of history and became a secret. But Muhsin Çelebi, once so rich, could never pay off the debts he had taken on for the caftan and redeem his farm, his dairy and his rents from mortgage. Selling the horse and the jeweled harness left to him as a keepsake of his embassy, he bought a tiny little garden in Kuzguncuk. He sowed it and reaped it. From it he earned bread for his wife and children. Until the day he died he sold vegetables in the Üsküdar market. He lived a very poor, very bitter, very deprived life. Yet he never bowed his head to anyone, nor did he ever win himself idle flattery by chattering about the sacrifice he had made when he threw his whole fortune to the ground in a single moment!

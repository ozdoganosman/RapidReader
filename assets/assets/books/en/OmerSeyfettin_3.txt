The Haunted Mansion
Ömer Seyfettin

Sermet Bey turned to the watchman behind him:

"Look, here's another empty mansion..."

He said.

A white, elegant building in front of a small pine wood. It gleamed so brightly it dazzled the eye, as though it were made of marble. Its flowerbeds were overrun with weeds. On the iron gate of its garden hung a large sign reading "For Rent." The watchman shook his head:

"Pass it by, sir, pass it by!... That place won't do for you."

"Why ever not, my good man?"

"Take the house I showed you a moment ago. It's small, but it's very lucky. Whoever lives there has a baby boy."

"How are twelve people supposed to fit into five rooms! Let's look at this one, this one... It's just right for us..."

The watchman, once again, with a firm gesture:

"You can't live here, sir..."

He said.

Sermet Bey could not take his eyes off the mansion. It had wide balconies on every side. You would have thought it was lounging on its foundations. It sat broad and squat, like a white Austrian hen brooding on her eggs. For twenty years, ever since he had been blessed with a family, he had always dreamed of a nest like this. With nervous impatience:

"Why can't we live here?" he asked.

"Sir, there's a ghost in this mansion."

"What ghost?"

"A ghost, plain and simple! It comes out at night. It gives the people in the house no peace."

Sermet Bey was not one of those who believe what their eyes see and their ears hear. Unless he could grip a thing firmly in his hand and feel it, he would not grant that it existed. To his mind, eyes and ears were nothing but hollows full of lies. Lies always got into us through these four doors. But the hand... the sense of touch was never taken in. All the superstitions and false beliefs ran to the eyes and ears to storm our minds. He laughed.

"A ghost can do us no harm!"

He said. The watchman stared into Sermet Bey's face as though he had heard a blasphemy.

"Everyone who moves in says that at first, but none of them lasts a month."

"What's it to you? Come on, let's look around the place."

"The owner has the key."

"Who's the owner?"

"The owner is Hacı Niyazi Efendi. The one who lives in that mansion next door..."

"Come on, let's get the key."

"All right, but..."

They turned back. They walked toward an old house painted red ochre, of which only the roof of the upper story could be seen through the dense trees.

On the way, the old watchman briefly told the history of the white mansion. For ten years, no one who had moved in had managed to stay more than a month. First the ghost would appear, then it would hurl great big stones, and at last it would come and break the windows, giving the people inside no peace at night. Two of the tenants had been struck down with heart attacks, the foster girls of three had been stricken by the spirits, and one tenant's wife had lost her six-month baby from fright. They passed beneath flowering almond trees in whose shade sheep were grazing. They knocked at the green door of the red mansion.

*****

Hacı Niyazi Efendi was a former official of the Pious Foundations. When the Constitution was proclaimed he had retired from his department with a severance payment and had begun to make his living buying and selling houses. But he was a very honest man. Though he sold perhaps a hundred houses a year, he would never palm his own haunted mansion off on some simpleton of a buyer from out of town who didn't know Hanya from Konya. "I fear God — why should I get mixed up in that!" he would say. He never hid the fact that his mansion was haunted. He opened the door himself. The watchman told him that Sermet Bey wanted to look over the house:

"Very well, come along!" he said.

He went ahead of them. They crossed the garden. Out in the street, Hacı Niyazi Efendi took a brass key from the pocket of his yellow homespun robe. He opened the garden gate. To Sermet Bey:

"This key opens the mansion too."

He said. They walked on; the garden really was rather wild. From neglect it had turned into an untrodden gully. In the little pine wood behind the mansion, too, there was a wild stillness. The watchman did not go into the mansion. He stayed at the gate. Sermet Bey went round with the owner. There was nothing at all to fault in the decoration. The ground floor was all marble. Cistern, bath, well, henhouse, stable... Everything was there.

"How much is the rent?"

"I'm not asking much. A hundred and eighty lira. But I want three years in advance."

"Why?"

"Here's why, sir. My enemies started this ghost talk so that the mansion would stand empty. The moment someone moves in, everyone starts spreading ghost stories for the sheer love of it. In the end the tenants think they have actually seen the lie they've heard. Then, say, in the middle of winter, they leave the mansion on my hands and clear off. Worse still, the ones who leave join the rumor-mongers too. If this goes on two more years, I'll neither be able to sell my property nor find a tenant."

Sermet Bey asked:

"How long has your mansion stood empty?"

"Well, in truth, hardly at all so far... But whoever moves in gets carried away by the neighbors' talk. They don't stay long. They take fright and run."

"I don't take fright."

"God willing."

"But three years in advance — that's a bit steep..."

"What can I do, sir? I've been burned. It's up to you..."

Sermet Bey liked the mansion very much. And the rent was cheap, too. These days they were asking a hundred and fifty lira a year for three-room hovels.

They drew up the contract that very day. The five hundred and forty lira of three years' rent was to be paid in advance. After they left Hacı Niyazi Efendi's house, Sermet Bey sent the watchman off with a twenty-five-kuruş note as a tip. The watchman spoke up:

"Your money's gone to waste, sir," he said. "You won't last three months, let alone three years."

"You'll see."

"We'll see. Hacı Efendi takes three years in advance from everyone who moves in, just like this, but not one of them lasts a single summer. And the money they paid goes up in smoke."

A week later Sermet Bey moved into the mansion with his large family. He was a true lover of pleasure. Every night there was music and merriment, eating and drinking, fun and revelry without end. There were always four or five guests from among his relatives, men and women. Sermet Bey was a Turk born and bred. But he had adopted the Europeans' maxim: "Toil by day, pleasure by night." His children went to school. He had placed his daughters as clerks in big commercial firms. His wife gave piano lessons at girls' schools. The only one in the house who did not work was his seventy-five-year-old mother. And she saw to the kitchen, the servants and so on. They ate dinner close to midnight, and after dinner they never sat up but went straight to bed. Not fifteen days had passed when one night a scream rang out from the ground floor. The maid Artemisya ran upstairs shrieking at the top of her lungs. She reported that something white was wandering among the pines out back.

"Your eyes must have played a trick on you!"

He said. Nor did they believe the other servants who had seen it. The whole family, young and old, went out onto the balcony of the back room. They saw the white phantom Artemisya was pointing at with her finger. It stood beneath the trees, as if looking at the mansion. Sermet Bey rubbed his eyes:

"Well, I'll be damned!" he said. "Just look at the power of suggestion!"

His wife, his daughters and his children went pale with fear. His eldest daughter spoke up:

"What suggestion, Father! It's right there in front of us — can't you see it?"

She said.

"I see it."

"Well then, what do you mean, suggestion?"

"Since the day we moved in, have we heard anything but ghost stories? Everyone who came had something to say. And now, under that influence, all of us at once are seeing something that isn't there."

"That's impossible."

"Why impossible?"

Sermet Bey told them how the conjurer Kazanöv had made an entire theater audience read the wrong time on the watches in their own pockets, and so on. "Our eyes see the lies that come in through our ears," he said, "but let us just lay a hand on this thing we see, and it will vanish at once." Then he got up. He paid no heed to his wife's protests. He dashed out into the garden to lay his hand on the apparition. He went toward the pines. But the phantom fled. It vanished. That night no one in the house could sleep but Sermet Bey.

... From then on they saw this phantom every night. Whenever Sermet Bey went out to lay a hand on it, the phantom fled. They began, somewhat, to get used to it. But one night, while they were all asleep, a tremendous jolt shook the mansion on its foundations. They ran to the balconies. They could see nothing. In the morning they found a huge stone at the foot of the dining-room wall. Sermet Bey's mother began telling him: "If you don't get us out of this mansion, I'll never forgive you." Two months' stay for five hundred and forty lira... That was not something that suited Sermet Bey at all. But every other night great big stones kept the household from sleeping and left them all in a state of alarm. Each time, Sermet Bey went after the phantom, but he could never manage to touch it. The neighbors, hearing that the stones had begun, would say: "If you don't move out soon, it'll break your windows too." Sermet Bey remembered the clause in the contract — "On vacating, all repairs are the responsibility of the tenant" — and grew all the more vexed, and thought of doing something before this window-breaking phase set in. Little by little his own conviction began to waver. At last they decided to move out. But they could not find another house. They began to hear a thousand more stories about the mansion. Supposedly the place had once been a graveyard. Where the kitchen now stood, a five-hundred-year-old saint lay buried... In spite of the stones thrown and the windows broken, Sermet Bey still did not believe in the ghost. This ghost always fled into the pine grove and vanished there. One day it occurred to Sermet Bey to hide in the pine grove and suddenly confront the ghost, or else to creep up quietly behind it and just lay a hand on it. No one in the household would agree to this: "It'll strike you down right there on the spot!" they said. But Sermet Bey, despite his troubled heart, simply could not bring himself to believe in ghosts, jinn and the like. The next evening he went into the grove. He climbed onto one of the lower branches of a big pine. He waited and waited. Midnight came. Those in the mansion could not sleep either, out of curiosity. He could see the poor souls pacing the balconies. Suddenly his heart leapt. The phantom had appeared.

Though he knew for certain that it would fly off and fade like a shadow the moment his hand touched it, Sermet Bey's knees began to shake all the same. "I'm not afraid, but my body is!" he said to himself. He jumped down quietly. He walked after the phantom. The lines of its shape looked very distinct. The phantom never heard him approaching. Slowly he stretched out his hand. He touched the white form. The phantom gave a terrible start. But it did not vanish. It turned, and on seeing Sermet Bey, it began to run for all it was worth.

Since it had not vanished at his touch, Sermet Bey understood at once that this phantom was no ghost or anything of the sort. He did not let it go. He gave chase. He caught it as it was climbing a plank propped against the low wall at the far end of the pine grove. He was very strong. The phantom, realizing there was no use resisting, stopped struggling. Sermet Bey growled:

"I'll teach you to make fools of people!"

With these words he hoisted the wretched phantom onto his back. He dragged it toward the mansion. He shouted:

"Bring a lamp! Let's see its face."

"..."

The household had come down to the garden gate.

"It's a man, the rascal! Didn't I always say there's no such thing in the world as jinn?"

The phantom would not let go of the white sheet over its head for anything. Sermet Bey pulled it off by force. They were astounded to see Hacı Niyazi Efendi, his beard all tangled up with his mustache. The poor wretch was covering his face with his hands so as not to show it. The damask nightgown he wore was torn at the back.

Sermet Bey burst out laughing.

The girls, the children and the servants stood gaping.

The old lady of the house,

"Why go frightening decent Muslim folk out of their wits, sir?..."

she asked. Sermet Bey answered for him:

"I know the reason for that!"

That was his reply. Then he told his eldest daughter to fetch the contract from the desk, quickly, along with pen and ink. Hacı Niyazi Efendi stood as if frozen, answering nothing he was asked, keeping his face turned toward the darkness. When the contract and the pen and ink arrived, Sermet Bey turned to him:

"Come on, then, take the pen in your hand!... Unless you want to pay for the people you've frightened into heart attacks and the babies you've made women lose, write what I tell you and sign it!"

He said. With a mechanical movement Hacı Niyazi Efendi snatched up the pen. Without a moment's hesitation he wrote down, word for word, what Sermet Bey dictated:

"I have received in advance from my tenant Sermet Bey the sum of one thousand and eighty lira, being six years' rent for the mansion."

"There, that's more like it!"

"..."

He signed it. Then, only half wrapped in his white sheet this time, he went off in the direction where he vanished every night.

Everyone was amazed that Sermet Bey had managed to live in the mansion for two years. Whenever the neighbors said to Hacı Niyazi Efendi:

"It seems the jinn in your house have moved somewhere else. Your new tenant doesn't look like he'll ever leave!" he would first turn pale, then flush red, and grumble this reply:

"No ablutions, no fasting, no prayers, no supplications... Men and women, children and all, they're drunk from dusk till dawn, every last one of them! Good heavens — never mind jinn, not even the Devil himself could show his face to that lot!"
